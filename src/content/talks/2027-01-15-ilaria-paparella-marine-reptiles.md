## Bio

Ilaria Paparella is the Curator of Fossil Marine Reptiles at the Royal Tyrrell Museum of Palaeontology in Drumheller, Alberta, since October 2024. Originally from Rome, Italy, she holds a Ph.D. in Earth Sciences from Sapienza University of Rome and a second Ph.D. in Biological Sciences from the University of Alberta. Her academic background also includes an M.Sc. in Earth Sciences and a B.Sc. in Geological Sciences, both from Sapienza University. Following her doctoral studies, she worked as an instructor at both the University of Alberta and MacEwan University in Edmonton, teaching courses in evolution, ecology, and vertebrate palaeontology. Her research focuses on the anatomy, evolution, and palaeobiology of Mesozoic marine reptiles. She uses comparative anatomy, phylogenetics, histology, and computed tomography to investigate their adaptations, evolutionary relationships, and palaeoecology.

## Abstract

Mosasaurs (Squamata, Mosasauridae) were a diverse group of secondarily aquatic lizards that dominated marine ecosystems during the Late Cretaceous. In a relatively short geological time, they developed highly specialized body plans for a fully aquatic lifestyle, including streamlined bodies, flipper-like limbs, and powerful, laterally compressed tails. Our understanding of mosasaur evolutionary history, diversity, and ecological adaptations relies heavily on the quality and completeness of fossil material. Over the past two decades, numerous well-preserved specimens have been recovered from various localities across Alberta, thanks to reports from the public and industrial sectors combined with ongoing field research.

To date, Alberta’s mosasaur record includes a considerable variety of specimens spanning different geological intervals and depositional environments. Examples of this rich record include an exceptionally high-latitude occurrence of tylosaurine material in Santonian–Campanian sediments of the Puskwaskau Formation near Grande Prairie, plioplatecarpine mosasaurs from the lower Maastrichtian St. Mary River Formation at Scabby Butte, as well as remains of mosasaurines, plioplatecarpines, and tylosaurines from the late Campanian Bearpaw Formation in the Dorothy and Dinosaur Provincial Park areas. However, some of the most remarkable findings have come from late Campanian shales of the Bearpaw Formation near Lethbridge, where excavations associated with ammolite mining operations have yielded exceptionally well-preserved skeletons. These include multiple specimens of _Mosasaurus missouriensis_, _Prognathodon overtoni_, and _Plioplatecarpus primaevus_.

The exceptional preservation of many Alberta specimens provides the opportunity to investigate aspects of mosasaur anatomy that remain poorly documented in the literature. While most previous research has focused on cranial morphology and dentition, relatively complete postcranial skeletons are providing new information on the evolution of the pectoral and pelvic girdles, vertebral column, and appendicular skeleton. These new anatomical data are being integrated into a phylogenetic framework, involving the development of a new morphological character dataset for the Mosasauridae. The addition of previously underrepresented postcranial characters, alongside a reassessment of existing morphological data, aims to improve our understanding of mosasaur diversity and interrelationships, as well as the evolutionary transformations associated with their transition to aquatic environments. Moreover, the study of taphonomic evidence, including scavenging traces and patterns of disarticulation, together with pathological conditions such as healed injuries, infections, and abnormal bone development, is providing insights into the biological interactions and environmental processes affecting these animals in life.

Overall, these remarkable specimens are not only expanding our knowledge of Alberta's Late Cretaceous marine ecosystems but are also providing valuable anatomical information that helps us better understand how mosasaurs evolved and diversified. They also show just how much Alberta's fossil record still has to offer and why continued fieldwork and research are so important.
